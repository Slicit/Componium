# Contributor License Agreement

## Why this exists

Componium is released under the GNU Affero General Public License v3.0, and is
also offered under separate commercial terms to organisations that do not want
the AGPL's obligations. That second option only remains possible while a single
party holds sufficient rights over the whole codebase.

If contributions arrive with no agreement in place, the project becomes jointly
owned by everyone who has ever contributed, and commercial licensing becomes
impossible without tracking down every one of them for permission. That
situation is irreversible in practice.

**This must be in place before the first external pull request is merged.**

## What a contributor grants

The intent, in plain terms:

- You keep the copyright in your contribution. You are not assigning it away.
- You grant the project a perpetual, worldwide, irrevocable copyright licence
  to use, modify, and distribute your contribution, **including the right to
  sublicense it** — which is what permits commercial relicensing.
- You grant an equivalent patent licence covering your contribution.
- You confirm you are entitled to make the contribution: it is your own work,
  and where your employer holds rights to what you write, you have their
  permission.

## Recommended instrument

Do not use bespoke text for this. Adopt the **Apache Individual Contributor
License Agreement (ICLA)** verbatim, with the receiving party changed from the
Apache Software Foundation to the copyright holder for Componium. It is widely
understood, contributors have usually signed one before, and its section 2
grant includes the sublicensing right that dual licensing depends on.

For contributors acting on behalf of a company, the Apache Corporate CLA (CCLA)
is the matching document.

Signature collection is best automated. CLA Assistant and similar GitHub apps
block merges until a contributor has signed, which removes the possibility of
merging an unsigned contribution by accident.

## Alternative worth considering

If a CLA proves to be too much friction for a hobbyist project, the fallback is
the Developer Certificate of Origin (DCO) plus an explicit statement in
`CONTRIBUTING.md` that contributions are licensed under AGPL-3.0 and that the
maintainer may relicense them. This is lighter, and materially weaker: it is a
declaration rather than a grant, and its enforceability for relicensing is not
settled the way the Apache ICLA's is.

---

**This document describes intent and is not legal advice.** Have the final
instrument reviewed by a lawyer before relying on it to sell a commercial
licence. The cost of getting this wrong is discovering years later that the
commercial option was never available.
